## Invent a small world

What could a few simple rules bring to life? A creature could seek shade, a crowd could avoid obstacles, or a tiny machine could react to your cursor. Choose a behaviour you're curious about; it doesn't need to become a game.

Play with <DemoLink id="ecosystem-simulation">Little world</DemoLink>. Change one condition, such as food or sensing, and watch how the creatures respond. Then imagine a rule the demo doesn't have.

### Separate the world from its picture

A simulation needs **state**, a **rule for change** and a **way to display it**. A position belongs to state; deciding where to move belongs to the rule; drawing a shape belongs to the display.

Describe one rule in plain language. What does an entity need to know to follow it? Position might be enough for bouncing off a wall. Seeking a target also needs information about that target. Start with only the state your rule uses.

Decide what happens at a boundary or when the goal is reached. Wrapping, bouncing and stopping create different worlds; none is the required answer.

### Find a starting point

Adapt the demo, [create an experiment](#/create) or use a project of your own. In `demos/ecosystem-simulation/`, explore:

- `world.ts`: how state advances without drawing the interface.
- `index.tsx`: how controls and rendering connect to the world.
- `world.test.ts`: small examples of what the rules should do.

Use the [SVG tutorial](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/SVG/Tutorial) to investigate drawing shapes and [requestAnimationFrame](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Window/requestAnimationFrame) for scheduling updates. The working demo is a reference, not a list of features to reproduce.

### Give time a meaning

Does your rule advance once per turn or continuously? For continuous movement, elapsed time matters: moving a fixed distance every frame ties speed to the screen's refresh rate.

Think about what should happen after a background tab resumes. A large time jump may be undesirable. Keep animation cleanup and a way to pause or restart while you explore; duplicate loops can look like a faulty movement rule.

### Probe your rule

Choose a few states whose next step you can predict: near an edge, at a target or between two competing targets. Check those before adding more entities.

Then change one condition. Does doubling speed change the behaviour you intended, or expose overshooting? Does adding another entity produce cooperation, competition or nothing at all?

Follow the result that interests you. A single surprising behaviour is enough; food, multiple creatures and player controls are optional directions.
